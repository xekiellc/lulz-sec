BARRETT BROWN
Barrett Lancaster Brown

Barrett Brown is the clearest case study in the criminalization of journalism in the digital age. A journalist and author who became a de facto spokesperson for Anonymous, he was indicted in 2012 on charges that included — most notoriously — sharing a hyperlink in an IRC chat room.

The link pointed to a file that had already been published by Anonymous after the Stratfor hack. The file contained credit card numbers. Brown did not hack Stratfor. He did not steal the credit card numbers. He did not publish them. He posted a link to a chat room where they were being discussed. For this, one of his original charges was identity theft.

At his peak he faced 105 years in federal prison. The charges were eventually reduced, but he still served 63 months — over five years — for conduct that in any reasonable interpretation of press freedom law constituted journalism.

He was released in 2016. He is free now. He is still writing. He was correct about everything he said about the private intelligence industry, about government surveillance, and about the prosecution of journalists who cover them too closely.

█ The Hyperlink Case

The legal theory behind Brown's identity theft charge was that by sharing a link to a file containing credit card numbers, he was trafficking in stolen financial information. Under this theory, any journalist who links to leaked documents containing personal information — a practice as old as investigative journalism — could be charged with identity theft.

The Electronic Frontier Foundation called the charges "a significant threat to the journalists who cover hacker groups." The Guardian, the New York Times, and numerous press freedom organizations expressed concern. The charges were eventually dropped, but not before Brown had spent years in pre-trial detention.

The remaining charges — obstruction of justice, related to a video he posted while his mother's home was being searched — still resulted in a 63-month sentence. The message to journalists covering Anonymous and related groups was clear: there is a price for this work.

█ Project PM

Before his arrest Brown ran Project PM — a crowdsourced research effort to analyze the documents released in the HBGary and Stratfor hacks. The goal was to map the private intelligence industry: the contractors, the relationships, the methods, and the clients.

The project produced some of the most detailed public analysis of the surveillance-industrial complex ever conducted. It was journalism conducted with the tools of the internet age — collaborative, open-source, and deeply threatening to powerful interests. Brown has suggested the prosecution was at least partly motivated by this work.

█ After Release

Brown was released in November 2016. He received the National Magazine Award for his columns written from prison for The Intercept. He has continued writing and speaking about press freedom, surveillance, and the private intelligence industry. He remains one of the sharper observers of the intersection between technology, power, and journalism.
