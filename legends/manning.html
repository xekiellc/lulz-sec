MANNING
Chelsea Elizabeth Manning

Chelsea Manning was a 22-year-old US Army intelligence analyst stationed in Iraq when she made one of the most consequential decisions in the history of whistleblowing. She had access to classified databases. She could see what was happening in the wars her country was fighting. And what she saw did not match what her country was telling its people.

She downloaded approximately 750,000 classified and sensitive documents and diplomatic cables and transmitted them to WikiLeaks. The material included the Collateral Murder video, the Iraq War Logs, the Afghan War Diary, and a trove of State Department cables that revealed the gap between American foreign policy as stated and as practiced.

She was arrested in May 2010, held in conditions that the UN Special Rapporteur on Torture described as cruel, inhuman, and degrading. She was convicted in 2013 and sentenced to 35 years — the longest sentence ever imposed in the United States for leaking information to the media.

President Obama commuted her sentence in January 2017. She served 7 years. In 2019 she was imprisoned again for refusing to testify before a grand jury investigating WikiLeaks — spending nearly a year in jail rather than cooperate with a prosecution she believed was unjust. She was released when the grand jury expired.

█ What She Revealed

The Collateral Murder video showed a US Apache helicopter crew in Baghdad killing 12 people including two Reuters journalists while laughing and joking. The crew requested and received permission to fire on people who came to help the wounded. Two children in a van were among those shot. The US military had previously claimed the journalists were killed in crossfire.

The Iraq War Logs documented 109,000 deaths in Iraq between 2004 and 2009, including 66,000 civilian deaths — far higher than official US figures. They also documented systematic torture of Iraqi detainees by Iraqi security forces, with US forces ordered not to investigate.

The Afghan War Diary documented civilian casualties, corrupt local officials on the US payroll, and covert operations in Pakistan. The State Department cables revealed diplomatic assessments that bore little resemblance to public statements — including frank descriptions of allied governments as corrupt, incompetent, or both.

█ The Conditions of Detention
Manning was held at Quantico Marine Base for nine months before trial in conditions of solitary confinement — 23 hours a day in a cell, forced to sleep without a pillow or sheets, required to stand at attention outside her cell. The UN Special Rapporteur on Torture formally found these conditions constituted cruel, inhuman, and degrading treatment. The Obama administration defended them. The State Department spokesman who publicly criticized the conditions resigned in protest.
█ After Release

Manning has remained publicly active since her release, speaking on trans rights, surveillance, and government transparency. She ran for US Senate in Maryland in 2018. She has written extensively about her experiences and continues to be one of the more visible figures in the whistleblower community.

Her case sits at the intersection of whistleblowing, trans rights, and military accountability in a way that makes it impossible to reduce to a single narrative. She is one of the most complicated and important figures of her generation.
