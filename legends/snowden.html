Edward Snowden was a systems engineer for the NSA — a contractor, not an employee, which gave him access to some of the most sensitive surveillance programs in the history of American intelligence. In June 2013 he gave journalists Glenn Greenwald and Laura Poitras a trove of classified documents and flew to Hong Kong.

What those documents revealed changed the world's understanding of digital privacy. The NSA was collecting phone records on every American. PRISM gave the government access to the servers of Google, Facebook, Apple, Microsoft, and others. The surveillance state that civil liberties advocates had warned about for years was not a conspiracy theory — it was infrastructure.

The US government charged him with theft of government property and two counts of violating the Espionage Act. He has been in exile in Russia ever since, unable to return home without facing prosecution.

He was right. The programs he exposed were later ruled illegal by a federal court. The journalism his disclosures enabled won a Pulitzer Prize. None of that got him home.

█ What He Revealed

The Snowden disclosures were not a single document dump — they were a sustained, carefully managed release of information conducted with professional journalists over months. The goal was not chaos but comprehension. Snowden wanted the public to understand what was being done in their name.

Key programs exposed: PRISM (collection from tech company servers), XKeyscore (global internet surveillance), MUSCULAR (tapping Google and Yahoo data centers), and bulk phone metadata collection under Section 215 of the Patriot Act. The phone metadata program was later ruled illegal by the Second Circuit Court of Appeals.

The disclosures also revealed surveillance partnerships with the UK (GCHQ), Canada, Australia, and New Zealand — the so-called Five Eyes intelligence alliance — and bulk interception programs targeting foreign leaders including German Chancellor Angela Merkel.

█ Life in Exile

Snowden has lived in Moscow since 2013, initially on temporary asylum and later as a permanent resident. He married journalist Lindsay Mills in 2017. In 2022 he was granted Russian citizenship.

He serves as president of the Freedom of the Press Foundation and remains active as a speaker, writer, and privacy advocate. His memoir Permanent Record was published in 2019 — the US government sued to seize the proceeds.

His situation is one of the defining ironies of the surveillance age: the man who revealed that the government was watching everyone is himself watched constantly, living in the country that watching made him flee to.
