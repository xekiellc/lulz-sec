WikiLeaksFounded 2006

Julian Assange founded WikiLeaks in 2006 with a simple and radical premise: transparency is a public good. If governments and corporations commit crimes in secret, publishing those secrets is journalism. The argument seemed straightforward. The consequences were not.

WikiLeaks published the Collateral Murder video — US military footage of an Apache helicopter killing Iraqi civilians and two Reuters journalists. It published the Afghan War Diary — 92,000 classified documents on the Afghan war. It published the Iraq War Logs — nearly 400,000 military reports documenting civilian casualties the US had claimed didn't exist. It published 250,000 State Department cables that reshaped global diplomacy.

The US government wanted him. He spent seven years in the Ecuadorian embassy in London claiming asylum. In 2019 Ecuador revoked his asylum and he was arrested by British police. He spent 1,901 days in Belmarsh maximum security prison while fighting extradition to the United States.

On June 25, 2024, he pleaded guilty to a single count of conspiracy to obtain and disclose national defense information. He was sentenced to time served and flew home to Australia — free for the first time in over a decade.

█ What WikiLeaks Published

The Collateral Murder video (2010) showed a US Apache helicopter crew killing 12 people in Baghdad including two Reuters journalists. The crew can be heard laughing. The US had previously claimed the journalists were killed in crossfire. The video proved otherwise.

The Afghan War Diary and Iraq War Logs revealed the systematic undercounting of civilian casualties, the existence of a secret US military unit tasked with killing or capturing high-value targets, and the torture of detainees by Iraqi security forces with US knowledge.

The State Department cables revealed what diplomats actually say in private — often dramatically different from their public positions. The cables reshaped relationships between countries, embarrassed governments worldwide, and provided the most comprehensive picture of American foreign policy ever made public.

█ The Press Freedom Argument
The core legal argument in Assange's case — and the one that press freedom organizations worldwide rallied behind — is that publishing classified government information is what journalists do. The New York Times published the Pentagon Papers. The Washington Post published NSA documents from Snowden. WikiLeaks published documents from Chelsea Manning. The Espionage Act charges against Assange, if upheld, would have created a precedent that any journalist who publishes classified information can be prosecuted. That is why the Committee to Protect Journalists, the Reporters Without Borders, the ACLU, and virtually every major press freedom organization opposed his extradition.
