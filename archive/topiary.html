Jake Davis grew up on Yell — one of the most remote inhabited islands in the British Isles, part of the Shetland archipelago off the northern tip of Scotland. Population: around 900. Internet connection: just enough to change the world.

Online he was Topiary — LulzSec's wordsmith, media strategist, and public face. He wrote every press release in Notepad without planning. He ran the Twitter account. He turned hacking into performance art. When LulzSec hacked PBS and posted a fake story claiming Tupac was alive in New Zealand, that was Topiary. When the CIA website went down and LulzSec tweeted "Tango down — cia.gov — for the lulz," that was Topiary.

He was arrested on July 27, 2011, 18 years old, on that remote Scottish island. Before his account went dark he deleted everything — every tweet, every post, every trace of the persona he had built. Everything except one.

"You cannot arrest an idea."

█ The Voice of LulzSec

What made LulzSec different from other hacking groups wasn't just the targets — it was the voice. Every release came with a manifesto, a joke, a provocation. Sony wasn't just hacked — it was mocked. The CIA wasn't just taken offline — it was trolled. Topiary was the reason LulzSec felt like a band rather than a criminal organization.

In a rare interview conducted just after LulzSec disbanded, Davis explained his approach: "I wrote every press release in notepad without planning. That's what made us unique, we just came out and made stuff up out of nowhere." The Lulz Boat name itself was an accident — he'd heard it referenced on a podcast and adopted it without knowing the Love Boat reference.

He described himself as "an internet denizen with a passion for change." That passion cost him two years of his late teenage years — 21 months on electronic tagging and 37 days in a young offenders' institution.

█ After LulzSec

Davis received a relatively lenient sentence given the scope of LulzSec's activities — a reflection of his age, his role as a communicator rather than a technical attacker, and the fact that he served his electronic tagging time before the sentence was handed down.

After release he moved into cybersecurity consulting and public speaking. He has appeared at security conferences, given interviews about the psychology of hacktivist movements, and remained thoughtful about what LulzSec meant and didn't mean. He has spoken about wanting to write a book about his experience. He remains one of the more publicly accessible former LulzSec members.
