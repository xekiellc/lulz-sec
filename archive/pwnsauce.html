Darren Martyn is the most quietly ironic figure in the LulzSec story. An Irish security professional who had built a legitimate reputation in the cybersecurity community — including serving as a chapter leader for OWASP, the Open Web Application Security Project, one of the most respected ethical security organizations in the world.

OWASP exists to improve software security through open standards, education, and community. Its chapter leaders are, by definition, people who believe in making the internet more secure through legitimate means. Martyn was one of them.

He resigned from his OWASP chapter leadership one week before his arrest as a LulzSec member. The timing suggests he knew what was coming. The resignation did nothing to soften the irony.

He was indicted on conspiracy charges on March 6, 2012, along with several other LulzSec members. The charges related to attacks on Fox, Sony, PBS, and other targets during the 50 Days of Lulz.

█ The OWASP Paradox

Martyn's dual life as both an OWASP chapter leader and a LulzSec member represents the blurriest version of the line between ethical and unethical hacking. OWASP's mission is to make software more secure. LulzSec's method of making software more secure was to break it publicly and embarrass the people responsible for securing it.

There's an argument — made by some in the security community — that LulzSec's public embarrassment of Sony, which stored over 1 million passwords in plaintext, did more for public awareness of basic security hygiene than years of OWASP guidelines. There's also an argument that breaking into systems without authorization is simply illegal regardless of the downstream effects.

Martyn sat at the intersection of both arguments. The fact that he was doing both simultaneously suggests he understood them both intimately.

█ After LulzSec

Martyn has maintained a lower public profile than his LulzSec colleagues since his legal proceedings concluded. He has been active in the Irish technology and security scene and has made occasional conference appearances, but has not sought the public platform that Sabu, Topiary, or Tflow have occupied.

His story is less dramatic than the others but no less interesting. The OWASP angle alone makes him one of the more complicated figures in a group that was full of complicated figures.
